\chapter{Factor regression and the meaning of
alpha}\label{factor-regression-and-the-meaning-of-alpha}}

This chapter defines the central estimand of the study --- the factor
alpha --- and derives, applies, and interprets the estimator that produces
it. The general least-squares and influence-weight results were
established in Chapter~\ref{preliminaries} (\S\ref{prelim-ols}); here they
are specialised to the three-factor model, extended to the sampling
distribution of alpha, and given an economic reading.

For portfolio $i$ in month $t$, the Fama--French three-factor (FF3)
regression is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ff3}
y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}\,(\mathrm{Mkt}\!-\!\mathrm{RF})_t
        + \beta_{i,S}\,\mathrm{SMB}_t + \beta_{i,H}\,\mathrm{HML}_t
        + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $y_{it}=r_{it}-r_{ft}$ is the portfolio's excess return and the three
regressors are the traded Fama--French factors introduced in
\S\ref{prelim-ff} and constructed in Chapter~4:
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  $(\mathrm{Mkt}\!-\!\mathrm{RF})_t$ is the aggregate market return in
  excess of the risk-free rate;
\item
  $\mathrm{SMB}_t$ (``small minus big'') is the size factor --- the return
  of small-capitalisation minus large-capitalisation portfolios;
\item
  $\mathrm{HML}_t$ (``high minus low'') is the value factor --- the return
  of high book-to-market (value) minus low book-to-market (growth)
  portfolios.
\end{itemize}
The coefficients $\beta_{i,M},\beta_{i,S},\beta_{i,H}$ are the portfolio's
sensitivities (loadings) to these factors, $\alpha_i$ is the intercept, and
$\varepsilon_{it}$ is the month-$t$ residual. Collecting a constant and the
$K=3$ factor series into the design matrix $X$ and writing
$\theta_i=(\alpha_i,\beta_i^{\top})^{\top}$, ordinary least squares chooses
the intercept and loadings that minimise the residual sum of squares,
giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ols-theta}
\hat\theta_i = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y_i,
\qquad \hat\theta_i=(\hat\alpha_i,\hat\beta_i^{\top})^{\top} .
\end{equation}

\hypertarget{deriving-ordinary-least-squares}{%
\section{Deriving ordinary least
squares}\label{deriving-ordinary-least-squares}}

This is the specialisation of the general derivation in \S\ref{prelim-ols};
it is repeated here in the factor-model notation because every later step
builds on it. For one portfolio, write $y=X\theta+\varepsilon$, where the
first column of $X$ is a column of ones and the remaining columns hold the
factor returns. The least-squares objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rss}
S(\theta) = (y-X\theta)^{\top}(y-X\theta)
          = y^{\top}y - 2\theta^{\top}X^{\top}y + \theta^{\top}X^{\top}X\theta .
\end{equation}
Differentiating and setting the gradient to zero gives the normal
equations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal-eq}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta} = -2X^{\top}y + 2X^{\top}X\theta = 0
\ \Longrightarrow\ X^{\top}X\hat\theta = X^{\top}y
\ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\theta = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y,
\end{equation}
and the Hessian $2X^{\top}X\succeq 0$ confirms a global minimum. The
residual vector is $e=y-X\hat\theta$, and the normal equations imply
$X^{\top}e=0$: the residuals are orthogonal to the intercept and to every
included factor \emph{in the estimation sample}. Orthogonality in-sample
is a mechanical property of OLS, not evidence that the model is correct.

\hypertarget{deriving-the-intercept-influence-weights}{%
\section{Deriving the intercept influence
weights}\label{deriving-the-intercept-influence-weights}}

Substituting $y=X\theta+\varepsilon$ into the estimator gives the
estimation error
\begin{equation}\label{eq:est-error}
\hat\theta-\theta = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\varepsilon .
\end{equation}
Let $q_0=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}$ select the intercept. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch5-influence}
\hat\alpha-\alpha = q_0^{\top}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\varepsilon
= a^{\top}\varepsilon = \sum_{t=1}^{T} a_t\,\varepsilon_t,
\qquad a^{\top}=q_0^{\top}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top},
\end{equation}
so $a$ is a fixed $T\times1$ vector of \emph{influence weights} that
depends only on the design $X$, not on the returns. Two facts follow that
matter for everything downstream. First, the intercept error is a
weighted sum of the residual shocks, which is the object whose variance
must be estimated (next section). Second, because all 25 portfolios share
the same factors, they share the \emph{same} weights $a$; applying them to
the same-month residual vector turns 25 separate errors into one jointly
distributed vector --- the bridge from OLS to the joint HAC covariance of
Chapter~6 (\S\ref{prelim-hac}).

\section{The sampling distribution and reported statistics of alpha}\label{alpha-sampling}

A point estimate is meaningless without its uncertainty. From
$\hat\alpha-\alpha=a^{\top}\varepsilon$ and the covariance rule
\eqref{eq:affine}, the variance of the estimated alpha is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-alpha}
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\alpha) = a^{\top}\Omega\,a,
\qquad \Omega=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon\mid X).
\end{equation}
Three cases make the content of \eqref{eq:var-alpha} concrete.

\emph{Classical case.} If the residuals were homoskedastic and serially
independent, $\Omega=\sigma^2 I$, then
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\alpha)=\sigma^2\,a^{\top}a=\sigma^2\,[(X^{\top}X)^{-1}]_{00}$.
In the centred single-factor toy below, $a_t=1/T$, so this is simply
$\sigma^2/T$ --- the familiar standard error of a sample mean.

\emph{Realistic case (HAC).} Monthly return residuals are heteroskedastic
and autocorrelated (Chapter~13 rejects the classical assumptions), so the
one-period formula understates uncertainty. Treating the scalar score
$s_t=a_t\varepsilon_t$ as a serially dependent series, the
heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent variance is the
long-run variance of \S\ref{prelim-hac} with Bartlett weights,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hac-alpha}
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\hat\alpha)
=\frac{T}{T-P}\Bigl\{\hat\gamma_0+2\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{l}{L+1}\Bigr)\hat\gamma_l\Bigr\},
\qquad \hat\gamma_l=\sum_{t=l+1}^{T} s_t s_{t-l},
\end{equation}
with $P=K+1$ and $L=12$. This is exactly the diagonal element of the joint
covariance of Chapter~6, and reproducing it against \texttt{statsmodels}
is the single-portfolio audit performed there.

\emph{Test statistics.} The HAC standard error is
$\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\hat\alpha)=\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\hat\alpha)}$,
and the individual test of $H_0:\alpha_i=0$ uses
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha-tstat}
t_i=\frac{\hat\alpha_i}{\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\hat\alpha_i)},
\qquad p_i = 2\,\Phi(-\lvert t_i\rvert),
\end{equation}
a large-sample normal reference; the 25 resulting $p$-values are then
false-discovery-controlled (\S\ref{prelim-fdr}). Two goodness-of-fit
summaries accompany each fit: the coefficient of determination
$R^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}$ (the share of the portfolio's return
variance explained by the factors) and the residual volatility
$\hat\sigma_\varepsilon=\operatorname{sd}(e)$.

\textbf{A standard error is not a return volatility.} The alpha standard
error measures how much $\hat\alpha$ would move across resamples of the
history; the residual volatility $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$ measures how much
the portfolio's monthly abnormal return scatters around the fit. They are
different quantities with different units of interpretation, and conflating
them is a frequent error: a portfolio can have a precisely estimated alpha
(small $\mathrm{SE}$) yet volatile residuals (large
$\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$), or vice versa.

\hypertarget{toy-example-interpretation}{%
\section{Interpretation}\label{toy-example-interpretation}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\ensuremath{\beta }:} sensitivity of the portfolio to a factor, conditional on
  the other included factors.
\item
  \textbf{\ensuremath{\alpha }:} average residual return after the included linear factor
  exposures are removed.
\item
  \textbf{\ensuremath{\varepsilon }:} month-specific residual, containing noise, omitted
  factors, nonlinearities, changing exposures and measurement error.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Alpha is not automatically skill.} For passive characteristic
portfolios, a non-zero alpha is better described as a pricing error or
benchmark-relative residual mean. The benchmark may be incomplete, so the
same interpretive caution as in \S\ref{rq-model-relative} applies:
$\hat\alpha$ measures return unexplained by \emph{these} factors, not by
every source of risk.

\textbf{What ``conditional on the other factors'' means.} The loading
$\beta_{i,H}$ on HML is not the simple regression slope of $y_i$ on HML; it
is a \emph{partial} slope. By the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem, $\hat\beta_{i,H}$
equals the slope from regressing $y_i$ on the residual of HML after HML has
itself been projected on the constant and the other factors
$(\mathrm{Mkt}\!-\!\mathrm{RF},\mathrm{SMB})$. Equivalently, partialling the
other regressors out of both sides isolates the sensitivity to the part of
HML that is orthogonal to the remaining factors. This is why a loading can
change --- even flip sign --- when a factor is added or removed, and it is
the mechanism behind the benchmark dependence of alpha discussed in
\S\ref{rq-difficulties}: moving from FF3 to FF5 re-partials every loading
and therefore re-defines the intercept.

\textbf{The constant-beta assumption.} Within one estimation window the
loadings $\beta_i$ are treated as constant. If the true exposures drift
over the window, the drift is absorbed into the residual $\varepsilon_{it}$,
where it can inflate residual dependence or masquerade as alpha. This is
one reason the study estimates on rolling windows (Chapter~10) and relies
on HAC inference rather than the classical variance: the linear,
constant-$\beta$ model is a local approximation, not a literal truth.

\hypertarget{reference-implementation}{%
\section{Reference implementation}\label{reference-implementation}}

\begin{verbatim}
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

def fit_factor_model(y, factors, hac_lags=12):
    X = sm.add_constant(np.asarray(factors, float))
    model = sm.OLS(np.asarray(y, float), X).fit()
    robust = model.get_robustcov_results(
        cov_type="HAC", maxlags=hac_lags, use_correction=True
    )
    return {
        "alpha_monthly": model.params[0],
        "alpha_bps_year": model.params[0] * 12 * 10_000,
        "betas": model.params[1:],
        "residuals": model.resid,
        "hac_se_alpha": robust.bse[0],
        "hac_p_alpha": robust.pvalues[0],
    }
\end{verbatim}

Independent replication means comparing the canonical estimator with a
separately implemented calculation such as statsmodels. Two functions
calling the same internal routine are not independent replication.
